ABSTRACT 

An interaction has two parts: someone decides to act, and then chooses whom to act on. Temporal link prediction has concentrated on the second, and we show that it is blind to the first by construction: a standard negative keeps the real source and swaps the destination, and we prove that this cancels the source’s activity exactly from the optimal score, so no model trained and evaluated this way is ever rewarded for learning it. Under the harder historical and inductive negatives, whose sources differ, the same factor becomes the dominant signal. We model it with Source Node Activity Modeling (SNAM) , a self-exciting event intensity fitted by an exact point-process likelihood to decayed interaction counts the his-tory states already contain; it has fewer than 20 parameters . On their own, never looking at the destination, these parameters beat DyGFormer and TPNet on four of five datasets under historical negatives. Added to the frozen scores of TPNet, TGN, DyGFormer and DSRD, four models of different design, without retraining anything , they raise AP on almost every backbone–dataset pair in both settings, by up to 25 points. Our full model ranks first overall against eleven base-lines on 13 datasets and three protocols, and on million-event streams trains an epoch 9–100 × faster than TPNet and DyGFormer. We conclude that source ac-tivity is a blind spot of temporal link prediction, and a cheap, transferable one to close. Code is available at https://github .com/Erutaner/Your-Temporal-Link-Predictor-Is-Blind-to-Who-Is-Active .

1arXiv:2610.04869v1 [cs.LG] 4 Oct 2026 Preprint. This is the setting of temporal link prediction. Existing studies learn interaction patterns through node memory, temporal neighborhood aggregation, and historical sequence encoding (Rossi et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025; Xiong & Sakellariou, 2026), and enhance relation modeling with temporal structure and candidate-specific information (Lu et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2025a; Tieu et al., 2025). These designs characterize which destinations are compatible with a source; a link discrimination objective never asks whether that source is about to act. This rate of acting is the conditional intensity of point processes (Hawkes, 1971; Zhou et al., 2026), here of the interactions a node initiates; a few temporal graph models use it as a training signal (Trivedi et al., 2019; Wen & Fang, 2022), but source activity has never entered the link score as a separate factor, for a reason: under a link objective alone it cancels between a positive and its random negative, so nothing rewards learning it; only negatives with other sources expose its weight. Benchmark studies agree: simple activity heuristics rival learned models (Daniluk & D ˛ abrowski, 2023; Cornell et al., 2025). We therefore formulate temporal link prediction as two factors, the source’s likelihood of acting now and the candidate’s compatibility, learned separately and combined. For the first factor we introduce Source Node Activity Modeling (SNAM). Using time-decayed interaction counts at K fixed timescales, SNAM estimates the source’s conditional intensity with only K + 1 parameters shared by all nodes. Its point-process likelihood fits this intensity to both the times at which a node acts and the intervals in which it does not, the latter never seen by a link objective. Because SNAM depends only on historical interaction counts and its own parameters, its score can be added to other link prediction models: with the same time unit and timescales on the same dataset, transfer requires only selecting the activity coefficient β on the backbone’s validation split, without retraining either model. The second factor completes the model. Multi-Timescale Historical Representation (MTHR) main-tains every node’s decayed states and interaction counts at the same K timescales, computed once per block of events, and feeds both factors. Candidate Relation Modeling (CRM) scores the candi-date’s compatibility from node-pair historical features and a candidate-conditioned encoding of the source’s recent interactions. The final score adds β times SNAM’s log intensity to the relation score; this is where source activity enters comparisons across sources. Historical and inductive negative sampling allow comparisons between candidate interactions in-volving different source nodes, aligning with the cross-source prediction needs of the motivating scenario; the related theoretical analysis is provided in Appendix A.1. We evaluate our model on 13 datasets. The full model achieves the best overall average rank across three negative sampling strategies, with its main advantages under historical and inductive negative sampling. Under histor-ical negative sampling, source activity scoring alone outperforms DyGFormer and TPNet on four of five datasets, with further improvements when combined with relation scoring. Cross-model transfer improves AP across all 20 backbone–dataset combinations, with gains of up to 25 percentage points. We make three contributions. (1) Diagnosis. A standard negative keeps the source and swaps the destination; we prove that the source’s activity cancels exactly from the optimal score, so the link objective never rewards it, which calls for learning it separately. (2) Method. SNAM does so with a point-process likelihood and K+1 parameters. Because it depends only on interaction counts, its log intensity can be added to other backbones’ scores with β chosen on validation and nothing retrained; the full model builds both factors on shared block-wise multi-timescale states. (3) Results. On 13 datasets against eleven baselines, SNAM alone beats DyGFormer and TPNet on four of five datasets under historical negatives and lifts four other backbones without retraining; the full model ranks first overall and, on long streams, is faster than TPNet and DyGFormer in training and inference. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Historical Representation and Relation Modeling. Existing methods encode temporal histories through walks, memory updates, and neighborhood aggregation (Wang et al., 2021; Kumar et al., 2019; Rossi et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2025; Xiong & Sakellariou, 2026), using sequence encoders, simplified networks, or state space models (Yu et al., 2023; Cong et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Tieu et al., 2025). Others capture structural relationships through common neighbors and temporal paths (Wang et al., 2021; Luo & Li, 2022; Souza et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025), or temporal patterns through time encoding and frequency analysis (Chung et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2024; Tieu et al., 2025), building on broader work on time representations (Kazemi et al., 2019; 2Preprint. Xu et al., 2019). DyGFormer and CRAFT capture source–candidate correlations through neighbor co-occurrence and cross-attention, respectively; CRAFT also uses destination recency in scoring (Yu et al., 2023; Yi et al., 2025a). We combine reusable historical aggregates with candidate-conditioned recent interaction encoding. 

Interaction Rates and Source Node Activity. Temporal point processes model event times con-ditioned on history (Zhou et al., 2026), using self-excitation, recurrent networks, and attention (Hawkes, 1971; Mei & Eisner, 2017; Zuo et al., 2020). Alternative formulations parameterize cu-mulative intensity or inter-event time distributions (Omi et al., 2019; Shchur et al., 2020). Graph methods define such intensities on node pairs from node representations and neighborhoods (Trivedi et al., 2017; Zuo et al., 2018; Trivedi et al., 2019; Wen & Fang, 2022); a node’s own rate is folded into the pair function. TREND (Wen & Fang, 2022) comes closest, adding a node-level term, but only as an auxiliary loss for the representation, not part of the link score. We model the source’s rate on its own, with K + 1 parameters over decayed interaction counts, so it enters the link score as a separate factor and transfers to other backbones on the same stream without retraining. 

Evaluation Protocols and Activity Information. Temporal graph benchmarks show that data splits, negative sampling, and evaluation procedures affect model comparisons (Poursafaei et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2023; Gastinger et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2025b; Dizaji et al., 2026); standardized evaluation is also emphasized in point-process benchmarking (Xue et al., 2024). Studies of global temporal dynamics, recency, and popularity further show that useful predictive information can be extracted without complex relation encoders (Daniluk & D ˛ abrowski, 2023; Cornell et al., 2025). We examine the complementarity between source intensity and relation scoring under different negative sampling protocols, distinguishing cross-source discrimination from candidate ranking for a fixed source.

4 METHODOLOGY 

Our model comprises three main modules: Multi-Timescale Historical Representation (MTHR), Candidate Relation Modeling (CRM), and Source Node Activity Modeling (SNAM). MTHR ag-gregates interaction histories across multiple timescales. CRM integrates these representations with candidate-specific historical information to quantify node-pair compatibility, while SNAM estimates the conditional intensity of source-initiated interactions from historical activity statistics. SNAM: Beyond an Integrated Component. SNAM learns source activity rates directly from interaction histories without relying on the internal representations of a specific relation encoder. Its parameters and training objective are independent of the other modules. This decoupled design enables SNAM to serve as a plug-in activity modeling component for existing link prediction mod-els: replacing f in equation 10 with the base model’s scoring function incorporates source activity information without retraining the base model. Furthermore, under consistent historical statistics, time units, and decay-scale configurations, trained SNAM parameters can be directly reused across models without model-specific retraining. SNAM can also be trained independently and use log λu(t) as its sole prediction score, allowing us to assess the discriminative power of source activity alone. We experimentally evaluate its cross-model reuse, effectiveness across datasets, and standalone predictive performance.

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS 

Datasets and Baselines. We evaluate on 13 temporal link prediction benchmarks: Wikipedia, Red-dit, MOOC, LastFM, Enron, Social Evo., UCI, Flights, Can. Parl., US Legis., UN Trade, UN Vote, and Contact (Poursafaei et al., 2022). We compare against 11 methods: JODIE (Kumar et al., 2019), TGN (Rossi et al., 2020), CAWN (Wang et al., 2021), EdgeBank (Poursafaei et al., 2022), Graph-Mixer (Cong et al., 2023), NAT (Luo & Li, 2022), PINT (Souza et al., 2022), DyGFormer (Yu et al., 2023), TPNet (Lu et al., 2024), GRN (Chang et al., 2026b), and DSRD (Chang et al., 2026a). Dataset statistics and baseline sources are in Tables 14 and 18. 

Task Settings. Following DyGLib, we split each dataset chronologically into approximately 70%/15%/15% for training/validation/testing and evaluate transductive and inductive link predic-tion, the latter involving nodes unseen during training. Training uses random negatives; evaluation uses random, historical, and inductive negatives with AP and AUC-ROC metrics. Detailed protocols are in Appendices E.2 and E.3. 

Model Configuration. Node embedding, low-rank matching, and random projection dimensions are 128, 16, and 256, respectively. We retain at most 20 recent interactions and 16 timescales, whose range is determined by the training interaction span and smallest positive inter-event interval. Dataset-specific timescales, block partitions, and other configurations are in Tables 14 and 17. 

Implementation and Optimization. We use AdamW with a base learning rate of 10 −3, and 10 −2

and 5 × 10 −2 for additional output projections and SNAM parameters, respectively. Training runs for at most 500 epochs with early stopping; checkpoints and the activity coefficient β are selected by validation performance. Each run uses one NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU. 5.2 OVERALL EFFECTIVENESS AND EFFICIENCY 

Predictive Performance. Table 1 reports transductive AP under three negative sampling strategies. Our full model (Ours +λ) achieves the best overall average rank of 2.44, primarily benefiting from historical and inductive negatives. SNAM improves the respective average ranks from 4.42 and 4.31 to 1.50 and 1.54, surpassing all baselines and supporting the complementarity of activity and relation scoring. Without SNAM, our model still outranks the strongest baseline, TPNet (4.33 vs. 4.55). Under random negatives, both configurations perform similarly without a clear advantage over the strongest baseline, consistent with the theoretical conditions discussed in Appendix A.1. Corrections to several baseline implementations (Appendix F.3) yield both performance increases and decreases. Additional results in Tables 15, 16 and 19, including inductive link prediction, show substantial gains under historical and inductive negatives on nearly all datasets.

our full-test-set scoring time, respectively. These results support the efficiency of shared historical aggregation and within-block reuse, with larger-dataset advantages further demonstrated in Figure 5 and Appendix G.4. Activity Term Coefficient β. On MOOC (Figure 2), transductive test AP peaks at β = 1 .0 under historical and inductive negatives, with no similar improvement under random negatives. SNAM leaves candidate rankings for a fixed source and timestamp unchanged, so nothing is lost on what random negatives test; the decline is an artifact of pooling sources in batch-level evaluation, whereas the two protocols that actually compare sources are the ones that gain. Appendix A.1 provides the formal analysis, and Figure 4 shows the remaining datasets. 

Number of Timescales K. Table 3 shows that moving from one timescale to several brings large gains, whereas adding more beyond about ten changes little. A moderate number of timescales therefore captures most of the benefit of multi-timescale historical modeling. 5.5 ABLATION STUDY 

Table 12 retrains the model without the candidate-conditioned recent interaction encoding, the random-projection structural features, the pair and endpoint counts, or all three. Dropping the en-coding or the projections changes the full score by under one point on every stream; dropping the counts costs up to two, and all three at most four once the activity term is in place. The advan-9Preprint. tage therefore lies in the multi-timescale backbone and the activity term, not in the engineered input features. 

6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

Random destination negatives preserve the source and time, cancelling their shared distribution from the optimal score and leaving absolute source activity unconstrained. Historical and inductive negatives allow different sources, making activity informative. SNAM learns source intensity with 

K + 1 parameters, outperforming DyGFormer and TPNet on four of five datasets under historical negatives. Added to four frozen backbones, it improves AP on almost every backbone–dataset pair by up to 25 percentage points. The full model ranks first overall against eleven baselines on 13 datasets and outpaces TPNet and DyGFormer on long streams. The cancellation argument is not specific to source activity: any factor that a negative sampler holds fixed between a positive and its negatives drops out of the optimal score, so the sampler decides what a model can learn and what a metric can see. Negatives drawn at the positive’s timestamp cancel global activity in the same way. This suggests auditing benchmarks for other cancelled factors, designing objectives and protocols around the comparisons a deployment actually makes, and learning such factors separately, as done here, rather than expecting the encoder to recover them.

A STATISTICAL MOTIVATION AND MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATIONS OF 

SOURCE ACTIVITY MODELING 

Our source activity model adopts a multi-timescale exponential Hawkes-type intensity (Hawkes, 1971). Unlike neural temporal-graph point-process models such as DyRep (Trivedi et al., 2019), however, it models source activity independently of learned relation representations, using only K +1 shared intensity parameters. For completeness, we derive the relevant point-process formulation and likelihood from first principles, without assuming prior familiarity with Hawkes processes. A.1 FROM NEGATIVE SAMPLING TO SOURCE ACTIVITY CORRECTION 

Temporal link prediction is usually formulated as follows: given the interaction history and a candi-date node pair, predict whether the pair will interact at the current time. However, a real interaction involves two different questions: Will the source node initiate an interaction at the current time? And, if it does, which destination node will it choose? The first question concerns the rate at which events occur, while the second concerns the conditional relationship between nodes. Separating these two factors is the starting point of Source Node Activity Modeling (SNAM). 14 Preprint. This distinction is closely related to how temporal link prediction models are trained and evaluated. In the DyGLib-style setting adopted in this work, a random destination negative sample (u, v −, t )

is constructed from a real interaction (u, v, t ) by keeping the source node and timestamp unchanged and replacing only the destination node. In contrast, historical and inductive negative sampling draw complete negative edges from their respective candidate sets. As a result, a negative sample may have a different source node. Although both settings are formulated as binary classification, they require the model to use different information. We first explain this distinction from a probabilistic perspective and then derive the motivation for adding an independent source-activity correction to the relation prediction score.

Scope of the cancellation. The blindness to source activity is created during training, not by the evaluation protocol. The cancellation in equation A.6 uses one property only: a training negative keeps the source and timestamp of its positive. Every method compared here trains this way, as do other benchmarks such as TGB (Huang et al., 2023), so the factor is never rewarded regardless of architecture; the historical and inductive protocols do not create the gap but merely make it visible, because they are the first comparisons across sources, which is also what a deployment ranks. The signature appears on all 13 datasets: under random negatives the validation split selects β = 0 in 123 of 130 cases (13 datasets, five seeds, two settings), whereas under historical and inductive negatives it selects β > 0 in 111 and 116. A pattern that repeats across thirteen streams of different domains, sizes and time resolutions is a property of the objective, not a quirk of one protocol or one dataset. Because the gap belongs to the objective rather than to any model, the remedy transfers: the same activity term raises every backbone it is added to. Nor is source activity special: any factor a sampler holds fixed between a positive and its negatives drops out of the optimal discriminator, a constraint for the design of objectives and evaluation protocols. A.2 THE SHARED MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATION OF SOURCE ACTIVITY AND 

MULTI -T IMESCALE HISTORICAL REPRESENTATION 

The previous section showed that conditional destination preference and source activity are two distinct factors in interaction prediction. Random destination negative sampling primarily supervises the former, while the latter can provide important additional information when an evaluation protocol allows negative samples to have different source nodes. This raises a natural question: if we need to estimate the source intensity λu(t) independently, must we design a separate temporal history encoder for it? One possible approach would be to use a recurrent neural network, temporal attention, or another se-quential model to encode the complete interaction history of each source node and predict its current activity rate from the resulting representation. However, this would introduce additional parameters and computational costs. It would also make the activity estimator dependent on a particular history encoder, limiting its ability to serve as an independent and reusable correction module. 20 Preprint. Our design takes advantage of a more direct connection: the historical aggregation used by Multi-Timescale Historical Representation (MTHR) captures not only information about past interaction partners. When the content of each interaction is set to a constant, the same aggregation operator yields the multi-timescale activity counts needed by Source Node Activity Modeling (SNAM). Thus, the historical states already maintained by MTHR contain the basic statistics required to construct SNAM. We only need to give these statistics an explicit interpretation in terms of event rates and learn how much each of them contributes to the current source intensity.

From multi-timescale activity counts to conditional event intensity. The next question is why these activity counts can be used to estimate the conditional event intensity λu(t) introduced in the previous section. The count nku(t) is not itself an event rate. A shared historical foundation with independent model parameters. We can now describe the relationship between MTHR and SNAM more precisely. Both modules use the same timescales ω1, . . . , ω K and extract information from the same strictly preceding interaction history. However, they use this information differently. MTHR aggregates interaction partners and their neighborhood information to learn node representations for relation 25 Preprint. prediction. SNAM turns the activity counts produced by the same historical aggregation operator into a conditional event intensity. The sharing takes place at the level of historical statistics, rather than by building the activity es-timator on top of MTHR’s trainable node representations. Given the interaction history and fixed decay rates, the counts nku(t) are already determined. They do not depend on the base node embed-ding table, the learned projections used in graph aggregation, or the parameters of the CRM relation scoring network. Therefore, the conditional intensity in equation A.15 depends only on the activity counts and its own K + 1 parameters. This property naturally connects to the statistical motivation established in the previous section. Since conditional destination preference and source activity are two different factors in the ideal probabilistic model, there is no need to force them to be produced by the same trainable represen-tation. Instead, sharing historical statistics avoids redundant computation, while separate parameter sets allow the two branches to receive supervision that matches their respective predictive objectives. For SNAM, this supervision does not require constructing any negative node pairs. The times at which real interactions occur provide evidence that the activity rate should be high, while intervals with no interactions also provide information that the predicted rate should not remain excessively high. In the next section, we derive the point-process likelihood of SNAM from these two sources of information. We further show that, because its intensity shares the same exponential kernels as the historical states, the time integral in its training objective can also be computed exactly. This allows us to learn source activity independently of the internal parameters of the relation branch, and provides the foundation for reusing the same activity model with other temporal link prediction backbones. The model now has three types of inputs for candidate relation prediction:

The parameters V1, V 2, Q 1, Q 2 are initialized to zero, so the additional pairwise and recent-interaction channels initially leave the output of the base scoring head unchanged and gradually contribute during training. We apply dropout with probability 0.2 to the concatenated node repre-sentations xuv and the complete-history pairwise features ηuv . Memory complexity. The model stores the global event index, the node-pair index, trainable node embeddings, and the fixed random projection matrix. Within a block, the main cached states com-prise the first- and second-order node aggregates, historical edge-feature aggregates, and first- and second-order random projections. (D.11) Additional memory is required for the current block’s candidate representations, historical records, recent-interaction features, and temporary computation tensors. During training, automatic differentiation retains only the computation graph of the current block. Unlike event-wise recurrent models with full BPTT, it does not need to retain an unfolded computa-tion graph spanning the historical event sequence. During inference, disabling gradient computation leaves the shared historical states and current query tensors as the main GPU memory components. Finer temporal partitioning reduces the num-ber of queries and within-block records processed at once, while the global indices and cached block-start states remain. In summary, shared block-start aggregation reduces repeated graph computation, fixed-depth matrix operations eliminate temporal BPTT, and closed-form intensity integration keeps SNAM inexpen-sive. The remaining costs are governed by block-start reconstruction, within-block historical record processing, and CRM’s candidate-conditioned features.

Each interaction stream is divided chronologically into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% test interactions, without randomly shuffling event order (Poursafaei et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2023). We evaluate both transductive and inductive link prediction using the same data partitions across the compared models. For inductive evaluation, we construct the unseen-node split used by DyGLib. We sample nodes that occur after the training cutoff, with the sampled set comprising 10% of all nodes, and exclude interactions involving these nodes from training. Validation and test interactions involving nodes absent from the resulting training graph form the corresponding inductive evaluation subsets. All compared models use the same node split and evaluation examples. 52 Preprint. Evaluation follows a streaming protocol: an interaction observed earlier in validation or test may enter the history of a subsequent query. History visibility is governed by event time rather than the training, validation, or test label. For a query at time t, interactions with te < t are available, while all interactions with te = t remain unavailable, irrespective of their file order. We enforce this rule for MTHR adjacency matrices and counts, CRM pair histories and recent interactions, and SNAM activity statistics. The Tied column in Table 14 measures the prevalence of precisely those timestamp ties for which file-order updates would differ from this rule. E.3 NEGATIVE SAMPLING AND BATCH -L EVEL EVALUATION 

During training, each observed interaction (u, v, t ) is paired with one random destination negative. During evaluation, we consider the random, historical, and inductive negative-sampling protocols of Poursafaei et al. (2022), using the DyGLib samplers (Yu et al., 2023). The latter two protocols replace easy, arbitrarily sampled non-edges with previously observed relationships and test whether a model can identify when such relationships occur or recur. Their distinction from random destina-tion sampling is also central to the statistical analysis in Appendix A.1. Let [bi, e i] be the timestamp range of evaluation batch i. Let Pi contain the pairs occurring in the sampler’s interaction stream within this interval, and let P≤bi contain the pairs with timestamps at or before bi. Finally, let P≤t0 contain the pairs observed by the cutoff t0 preceding the current evaluation period. The sampler operates on the complete evaluation stream in the transductive setting and on the corresponding new-node subset in the inductive setting. 

Random negative sampling. For each positive interaction (u, v, t ), the random sampler preserves its source and timestamp and replaces only the destination:

Our evaluation uses the strictly preceding history 

Ht− = {e : te < t },

as defined in Appendix E.2. We identified several ways in which released baseline implementations could expose information from the query’s own timestamp or label, or allow one query’s compu-tation to depend on other examples in the same batch. We corrected these information paths while retaining the original model architectures, objectives, and training configurations. Corrections af-fecting training are applied in both training and evaluation, followed by retraining from initialization. All modifications are marked in the accompanying source code to facilitate independent inspection. 

TPNet: delayed commitment of simultaneous events. TPNet maintains temporal random-projection states and updates them after scoring each batch. In the released implementation, an entire scored batch is immediately incorporated into the states used by the next batch. When two consecutive batches contain events with the same timestamp, a query in the second batch can there-fore access simultaneous events from the first batch. We replace immediate state updates with delayed commitment. Scored events are placed in a pend-ing buffer. Before scoring a new batch whose first timestamp is t, only buffered events satisfying 

te < t are committed through TPNet’s original update operator. Events with te ≥ t remain pending until the query time advances beyond them. This preserves TPNet’s state representation and update equations while preventing information from simultaneous events from crossing batch boundaries. Remaining buffered events are committed when the training epoch or evaluation stream ends. 

DyGFormer: eliminating label-correlated padding. DyGFormer encodes recent interaction se-quences using temporal patches and pooled sequence representations. In the released implemen-tation, each model call pads its sequences to the maximum length appearing in that call. Positive and negative examples are evaluated in separate calls, whose historical sequence-length distributions may differ. Because padded positions participate in the subsequent unmasked pooling operation, the padding length can influence the resulting representations. Separate positive and negative calls therefore introduce a label-correlated feature unrelated to the individual query’s history. We set the padding length of every call to the maximum sequence length L specified by the released configuration. The historical sequence truncation, padding values, network architecture, and pooling operation remain unchanged. Every query is consequently encoded using the same input length, independently of the other examples in its model call. 

GRN: consistent supervision and causal state updates. We corrected six issues in the released GRN training and evaluation pipeline, organized into three parts. First, we align predictions with their supervision. The released training loop used the sampled negative destination in the positive branch, rather than the true interaction destination. We restore the true pair (u, v ) as the positive example and use (u, v −) only as the negative example. Features of the interaction being predicted are also removed from the scoring inputs; observed event features enter the model only when their events are committed to the historical state. Second, we enforce causal history and consistent state access. The released within-chunk mask was based on record order and could expose earlier rows with the same timestamp. Moreover, updates 57 Preprint. performed by the positive branch could influence the negative branch, while scoring a negative pair could overwrite the sampled destination’s last-interaction time. We replace the row-order mask with a strict temporal condition, score positive and negative candidates against the same committed history, and prohibit negative examples from modifying historical states. Newly observed positive events become available only to queries with strictly later timestamps. Third, we correct state restoration and dataset loading. The original state backups retained references to tensors subsequently modified in place, so restoring them did not reliably recover the intended historical state. We create independent state copies and reconstruct the training-end history by re-playing training interactions under the selected checkpoint before validation or test evaluation. We also retain all appropriate transductive validation and test events, including those involving nodes held out for inductive evaluation, and provide historical and inductive negative-sampling evaluation using the DyGLib protocols. These changes leave GRN’s retention architecture and loss formulation intact while ensuring that its predictions are based on the intended historical information. 

DSRD: separating the local subgraphs of different queries. DSRD constructs a joint subgraph from the center nodes and sampled temporal neighbors of a batch. The released construction con-catenated center-node IDs and neighbor occurrences, then mapped global node IDs to subgraph rows. When a center node also appeared as another query’s neighbor, repeated IDs could be mapped to the same row, causing historical information from different queries to be combined unintention-ally. Because positive and negative examples were scored in separate calls, the resulting collisions could also depend on the composition and label of the batch. We assign separate local subgraph rows to every center node and every sampled neighbor occur-rence, and connect each historical edge to the representation belonging to its own query. The re-sulting subgraph of a candidate depends on that candidate’s sampled history rather than on the other candidates in the batch. We additionally align the unseen-node sampling order with DyGLib and retain the complete trans-ductive validation and test event sets. 

Memory-based models: causal message commitment. The released memory-update implemen-tations used by JODIE (Kumar et al., 2019), DyRep (Trivedi et al., 2019), and TGN (Rossi et al., 2020) may aggregate pending messages from the query’s own timestamp when consecutive batches share that timestamp. We apply the same strict temporal commitment principle used for TPNet: messages are incorporated into readable memory only when their timestamps precede the current batch’s first query time. The main comparison tables retain the attributed published results for models that were not retrained in this study. For SNAM transfer, we use TGN trained with the corrected memory implementation. The corresponding corrected source is included with the other baseline modifications. F.4 TRANSFERRING SNAM TO OTHER MODELS 

We evaluate SNAM as a reusable activity component by applying it to TPNet, TGN, DyGFormer, and DSRD on Wikipedia, Reddit, UCI, Enron, and LastFM. The results are reported in Table 2. We first obtain independently trained backbone checkpoints using each model’s released architec-ture, dataset configuration, and early-stopping procedure, with the relevant corrections from Ap-pendix F.3. These training runs provide the backbone checkpoints needed for evaluation where pretrained checkpoints were unavailable. After training, every backbone is frozen: adding SNAM does not require another backbone training run or any change to the backbone’s parameters. For each dataset, we reuse the SNAM parameters 

ϑ = ( θμ, θ 1, . . . , θ K )

from the seed-0 checkpoint of our own model on that dataset. An independent historical-counting procedure computes the source activity of every evaluated positive and negative pair, using the same 58 Preprint. time units, exponential timescales, and strictly preceding history as our model. In particular, a historical or inductive negative pair receives the activity rate of its own source node. For each backbone, we record the link predictor’s output before the sigmoid, together with the source node and query time of every evaluated example. The transferred score is 

sβ (u, v, t ) = fbase (u, v, t ) + β log λu(t).

Here, fbase is the frozen backbone’s original logit. Neither the pretrained SNAM parameters nor the backbone parameters are updated during transfer. We select 

β ∈ { 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4}

by maximizing validation AP separately for each dataset, backbone, prediction setting, and negative-sampling protocol. The selected coefficient is applied unchanged to the corresponding test split. AP and AUC are computed using the batch-level evaluation procedure of Appendix E.3. 

Historical state at the validation boundary. For backbones that maintain persistent historical states, selecting β requires validation predictions generated from the state at the end of training, before any validation events have been consumed. DyGFormer has no persistent cross-batch memory. For TGN, we restore the training-end memory saved during training. For TPNet, we replay training events through its parameter-free historical update to reconstruct the training-end state. For DSRD, we restore the corresponding saved node state. Each backbone then follows its original streaming evaluation procedure. As a consistency check, setting β = 0 in the transfer pipeline reproduces the backbone-only eval-uation results. Tables 15, 16, and 19 supplement the results in the main-text Table 1. The advantage under hard negative sampling extends to both unseen-node prediction and different evaluation metrics. In the inductive setting, Ours using only the relation score attains an average rank of 4.81 under both historical and inductive negative sampling, outperforming all comparison baselines. Adding SNAM improves both average ranks to 2.35 and achieves the highest AP on 9 of the 13 datasets under each protocol. For example, under historical negative sampling, its AP on MOOC reaches 95.54%, exceeding the strongest baseline, TPNet, by 13.69 points. TPNet remains stronger under random negative sampling; our competitiveness also includes the efficiency gains from shared historical computation, as discussed in Section G.4. The AUC results exhibit the same overall trend. In the transductive setting, the full model attains average ranks of 1.50 and 1.62 under historical and inductive negative sampling, respectively. Across all three pro-tocols, the full model achieves overall average ranks of 2.44, 3.18, 2.46, and 3.33 for transductive AP, inductive AP, transductive AUC, and inductive AUC, respectively, ranking first in every table. G.2 COMPLETE TRANSFER RESULTS 

Table 9 extends the transfer study in the main-text Table 2 to additional protocols. TPNet, TGN, DyGFormer, and DSRD represent different relation-modeling architectures, while SNAM contains only 15–17 learnable parameters on these five datasets. Transfer directly reuses the SNAM param-eters already learned on the same dataset, freezes both the activity model and the target backbone, 60 Preprint. and selects only the fusion coefficient β on the validation split; neither component is retrained. The detailed procedure is provided in Appendix F.4. Across the two settings in the supplementary table, all 40 comparisons spanning four backbones and five datasets yield AP improvements. In the transductive setting under inductive negative sampling, the average gain across datasets ranges from 5.86 to 12.28 points across backbones; in the inductive setting under historical negative sampling, it ranges from 6.42 to 17.27 points. In the latter setting, DyGFormer improves from 63.40% to 88.51% on Wikipedia. These results show that source-activity information complements the relation representations learned by different backbones and that its benefit does not depend on our relation encoder. Table 6 further covers the remaining eight datasets. Among the 24 reported comparisons under hard negative sampling, 19 improve and 4 remain unchanged; the only decrease is 2.42 points on UN Vote in the inductive setting. On UN Trade, the gain reaches 25.75 points in the transductive setting under inductive negative sampling. This extended study uses one seed per dataset. Overall, the results support source activity as a modeling dimension complementary to relation encoding. They also suggest that future work should distinguish between the two prediction questions—whether a source is active and which destination it selects—when designing training negative sampling, loss functions, and evaluation protocols. G.3 ROLES OF THE INDIVIDUAL MODULES 

Multi-timescale historical representation. Table 10 shows that multiple timescales generally pro-vide substantial gains over a single timescale, whereas further densifying the timescale grid yields smaller improvements. For example, in the transductive setting under historical negative sampling, the AP of the full model on UCI increases from 79.48% at K = 1 to 95.43% at K = 8 , and reaches 96.12% at K = 28 ; on Reddit, it remains between 90.01% and 90.16% for K = 11 –32 . Cover-ing different temporal ranges is therefore more important than continually increasing the number of timescales. This trend does not hold for every individual result: for example, in the inductive setting under historical negative sampling, Wikipedia performs better at K = 1 . The default timescales are determined uniformly from the temporal range of the training data, as described in Appendix B.4. 

Within-block updates. Table 7 shows that, when within-block updates are retained, the full model is generally robust to changes in the number of blocks C; disabling these updates substantially weakens the benefit of the activity term. Even at C = 192 , AP under the transductive historical protocol decreases by 2.13–5.30 points across the five datasets. Together with Appendix D.2, these results show the importance of retaining within-block events that have occurred before the query time, so that the activity counts are updated exactly to the current time. This exactness applies to the exponential counts and does not imply that all higher-order representations are recomputed at every query. 

Inputs to the relation branch. Table 12 shows that removing the recent-interaction encoding or the random-projection features produces only small changes on several datasets, indicating that the performance does not depend on any single auxiliary branch. The count features and the joint contribution of the different inputs are more consequential. For example, removing all three input groups on UCI reduces transductive random AP from 95.81% to 83.09%. The results therefore support the complementary roles of multi-timescale historical representations and candidate-specific relation features, rather than suggesting that all inputs can be omitted. In this table, “no projections” also removes the recent-interaction encoding that depends on those projections and should therefore be interpreted as a joint ablation. 

Form of the activity term. Table 8 and the main-text Table 5 show that recency and the MLP also yield substantial gains, supporting the value of activity information itself. Under the transductive historical protocol, the MLP exceeds SNAM by 0.17–0.35 points, but it is not consistently better in the other settings. This MLP uses the same historical counts as SNAM, takes log(1 + nu) as input, and adopts a two-layer K → 32 → 1 architecture with GELU activations. During training only, it treats the source nodes of real events as positives and source nodes sampled randomly from the training set at the same timestamps as negatives, fits a binary cross-entropy objective, and is then frozen and used in place of the activity score. 61 Preprint. We adopt SNAM because it requires only K + 1 parameters, admits a clear event-intensity interpre-tation, and shares exponential historical statistics with the backbone. Its point-process likelihood has a closed-form integral and requires neither additional negative sampling nor numerical integration (Appendices A.2 and A.3). In the main-text Table 4, the activity score alone outperforms TPNet and DyGFormer on four of the five datasets, further demonstrating its independent predictive value.

Event-stream length. In Table 13, a tenfold longer LastFM stream costs 1.7 × the epoch time (12.6 s to 21.7 s) and 5.5 × the scoring time for 10 × the test positives, so the cost per event and per query falls with length; with C fixed at 192, the short prefix pays a high per-example overhead. On Flights the number of blocks grows with the stream (5 to 90) and the epoch time grows with it (0.5 s to 13.8 s), while the scoring time stays at about 1 s per 10 5 positives. This matches the cost structure of Appendix D.4: an epoch costs a fixed block-start term plus a term linear in the number of events, and a query reads a state of fixed size plus the events of its own block, so its cost depends on the block size rather than on the length of the history.
