# FireGen: Quantifying Wildfire Risk by Simulation 

Allyson Hineman 1, William Kleiber 2, Stephan Sain 3, and Alexis Hoffman 4

July 20, 2026 

Abstract 

Wildfire regimes in Northern California exhibit strong spatio-temporal variability, heavy-tailed fire size distributions, and sensitivity to climatic conditions. We develop a hierarchical statistical framework to model wildfire occurrence, geometry, and burned area in Northern California from 1984-2023. Fire centroids are modeled as a spatio-temporal point process with covariate-driven intensity, extended by a multiplicative gamma-based stochastic shock to better capture the distribution of monthly fire counts. Conditional on fire location, burned area polygons are represented using a parametric ellipse model that separates scale, shape, and orientation, with ellipse parameters estimated from standardized fire perimeters. Total burned area is modeled using a heteroskedastic lognormal regression incorporating climatic and spatial covariates, including vapor pressure deficit anomalies. The framework enables Monte Carlo simulation of wildfire processes and provides a probabilistic tool for assessing wildfire risk and cumulative burned area under observed climate variability. Keywords: Wildfire modeling; spatio-temporal point processes; hierarchical models; ellipse approximation; lognormal regression; Monte Carlo simulation

This inherent variability makes probabilistic methods well suited for wildfire risk assessment (Reed and Hughes, 2002), highlighting that aggregate wildfire impacts are dominated by extreme events. 

# 1.1 Mechanistic and Statistical Wildfire Modeling 

Mechanistic approaches to wildfire modeling explicitly simulate the processes governing wildfire spread. These models typically integrate fuels, weather, and topographic information to represent fire behavior (Finney, 2006). Mechanistic modeling encompasses physical, empirical, and stochas-2tic representations of fire dynamics (Sullivan, 2009a,b,c). Recent developments have incorporated probabilistic representations of uncertainty through Bayesian and stochastic fire spread models (Yoo and Wikle, 2024; Grieshop and Wikle, 2024). Related frameworks such as FSim address large-scale risk assessment by simulating thousands of hypothetical fire seasons, but similarly require extensive environmental data and computational resources (Finney et al., 2011). Statistical models have played a central role in large-scale wildfire analysis by relating wildfire occurrence and burned area to climatic and environmental drivers. Such approaches have demon-strated strong links between climate variability, anthropogenic warming, and wildfire activity across diverse ecosystems (Preisler et al., 2004; Littell et al., 2009; Turco et al., 2018). These studies show that statistical models can effectively capture broad climate-fire relationships without explicitly resolving fire spread dynamics. More recently, machine learning and deep learning methods have been applied to wildfire prediction and climate-fire interaction modeling, including work by Richards et al. (2023) on extreme Mediterranean wildfires. However, many such approaches prioritize predictive accuracy over generative consistency and are not designed to produce coherent stochastic realizations of wildfire events or full probabilistic representations of uncertainty. A complementary statistical framework for wildfire occurrence is based on spatial and spatio-temporal point processes. Inhomogeneous Poisson point process models allow wildfire intensity to vary continuously over space and time as a function of environmental and climatic drivers (Diggle, 2013; Baddeley et al., 2015), and such models have been widely applied to wildfire ignition and occurrence data (Hering et al., 2009; Serra et al., 2014). Notably, wildfire occurrence often exhibits clustering and variability beyond standard Poisson assumptions, so stochastic extensions such as Cox processes, log-Gaussian Cox processes, and hierarchical Bayesian models have been developed to represent latent environmental variability and multiscale dependence (Cox, 1955; Møller et al., 1998; Diggle et al., 2013; Wikle, 2003; Banerjee et al., 2014). Separate statistical models have also been developed for wildfire burned area distributions. Due 3to the inherent variability, uncertainty, and complex behavior of wildfire growth, burned area is commonly modeled using statistical distributions with heavy tails to capture the extreme skewness of observed fire sizes (Ricotta et al., 1999; Reed and McKelvey, 2002; Schoenberg et al., 2003; Cumming, 2001; Joseph et al., 2019). Further work shows that incorporating climate variability and drought indices improves explanation of regional fire size dynamics (Barbero et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2020). In particular, vapor pressure deficit has emerged as a key driver linking atmospheric dryness to both ignition probability and fire growth potential (Abatzoglou and Williams, 2016; Williams et al., 2019). Despite these advances, wildfire occurrence and burned area are often modeled separately rather than within a unified generative framework. In addition, relatively few statistical wildfire models explicitly generate spatial burned area polygons suitable for simulation-based risk analysis. Many instead reduce fire outcomes to scalar summaries such as counts or total burned area, limiting their usefulness for exposure and impact modeling. Simulation frameworks that generate ensembles of full wildfire realizations provide a natural alternative, preserving spatial dependence while enabling Monte Carlo estimation of downstream risk (Williams et al., 2026). 

# 1.2 Overview of This Study 

This study focuses on generative models of wildfire characteristics, including locations, burned area and burn pattern geometry. This should be contrasted against predictive approaches that focus on wildfire spread that can be used in real-time forecasting. Our goal is to capture distributional behavior of fire that can be used in offline risk assessment efforts. Wildfire occurrence is mod-eled as a spatio-temporal point process whose intensity depends on environmental covariates and seasonal forcing. To account for extreme wildfire months beyond what is captured by determin-istic climate covariates alone, we introduce a multiplicative stochastic shock process that varies over time. Conditional on wildfire occurrence, burned area polygons are represented through a simplified geometric approximation in which fire perimeters are modeled as randomly-oriented 4ellipses with stochastic area and shape parameters. This representation separates wildfire geom-etry into interpretable components, including orientation, elongation, and scale, while remaining computationally tractable for large-scale simulation. The framework is illustrated using wildfire perimeter data together with climate covariates. It supports direct simulation of complete wildfire realizations under observed or future climate conditions, enabling probabilistic characterization of wildfire risk and its associated uncertainty over time. Our approach complements recent stochastic simulators such as WULFFSS (Williams et al., 2026), which simulate wildfire activity at a monthly grid-cell level, by modeling individual wildfire events and jointly generating their occurrence, burned area, and spatial geometry within a unified hierarchical statistical model. By generating ensembles of plausible wildfire realizations, our model captures both typical wildfire behavior and rare high-impact events while preserving event-level spatial structure relevant for risk assessment and impact modeling. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the study region and wildfire and other datasets used in the analysis. Section 3 presents the hierarchical statistical framework for wildfire occurrence, burned area, and geometric fire representation together with estimation procedures. Section 4 evaluates model performance and illustrates simulation behav-ior across Northern California. Finally, Section 5 concludes with discussion of the framework, limitations, and directions for future work.

# 2.2 MTBS 

Wildfire occurrence and burned area data are obtained from the Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity (MTBS) dataset, which provides spatially explicit fire perimeter polygons and ignition dates for large fires across the United States (U.S. Geological Survey and U.S. Forest Service, 2025). In the western U.S., MTBS defines a fire as an event burning at least 1,000 acres (approximately 405 ha), while in the eastern U.S., the threshold is 500 acres (approximately 202 ha). For this study, we restrict fires to the Northern California subregion defined in Section 2.1 during 1984–2023. Only fires classified by MTBS as wildfire or unknown are retained; prescribed 

6burns and wildland fire use incidents are excluded to focus on unplanned wildfire events. Wildfire characteristics include ignition date, burned area and location defined by the polygon’s centroid. 

# 2.3 ERA5 

Climate covariates are obtained from the ECMWF Reanalysis 5th Generation ERA5 product, a global atmospheric dataset produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Fore-casts (Hersbach et al., 2020). Monthly fields of mean temperature, maximum temperature, wind speed, total precipitation, mean and minimum relative humidity, standardized precipitation evapo-transpiration index (SPEI), and vapor pressure deficit (VPD) are extracted at 0 .25 ◦ × 0.25 ◦ spatial resolution and aggregated to each pyrome by averaging grid-cell values within the pyrome boundary. For each climate variable, monthly climatologies are calculated by averaging values from 1983– 2024 within each calendar month. Climate anomalies are then defined as departures from these monthly climatologies. In particular, VPD anomalies represent deviations from the long-term monthly mean, providing a measure of anomalous atmospheric moisture demand. To account for delayed wildfire responses, lagged climate metrics are also computed as averages over the preceding one-, two-, and three-month periods. To assess sensitivity to different representations of atmospheric moisture demand, additional candidate predictors include multiple summaries of monthly VPD, including the mean, minimum, maximum, median, and 25th and 75th percentile values. 

# 2.4 Elevation 

Elevation is included as a spatial covariate to capture topographic influences on wildfire occur-rence and behavior. Higher or lower elevations can affect vegetation types, fuel availability, and microclimate conditions, all of which may influence fire risk and spread. In this section, we detail a statistical model for wildfire burned areas, a proposed estimation procedure, and how the model can be used to simulate burned area polygons under different climate conditions. The model includes distinct components for fire occurrence (location) and fire size (burned area). We refer to ‘burned area’ as the spatial extent of a wildfire event, represented as a polygon in geospatial data. 

# 3.1 Model 

We propose a hierarchical approach to modeling wildfire burned area polygons, consistent with general hierarchical spatial modeling frameworks (Wikle, 2003; Banerjee et al., 2014). At the first level, we model the spatio-temporal distribution of fire event locations represented by the centroids of burned area polygons. For each event, we assume the resulting burned area polygon is approximately elliptical, a simplifying assumption sometimes used in wildfire spread modeling (Anderson, 1983). Conditional on time and location, the second level specifies a simple geometric model for the shape of the burned area polygon consistent with this elliptical assumption, and the final layer simulates the total area burned.

Initial calibration based solely on weighted quantile distance produces simulations that inadequately reproduce other important distri-butional characteristics, including total counts, sparsity, and tail behavior. Additional discrepancy metrics are incorporated sequentially until the simulated distributions provide a better represen-tation of the observed distribution across extreme-event frequency, central tendency, sparsity, and tail behavior (Malamud et al., 1998; Reed and McKelvey, 2002; Schoenberg et al., 2003). We treat the weights (𝑤 1, . . . , 𝑤 5) in 𝐺𝑂𝐹 total (𝛼, 𝛾 ) as spatially-dependent hyperparameters. To propose different weight vectors per spatial region, we use a space-filling design, producing a diverse set of candidate weight vectors. For each candidate vector, we evaluate model performance using 4-fold cross-validation across decades: for each fold, we simulate counts over a grid of plausible (𝛼, 𝛾 ) values and compute the normalized discrepancy components. These components are combined according to the candidate weights to yield a fold-specific fit score. Aggregating the mean total fire count errors across folds and across multiple data subsets, we have a grid of plausible parameters to which we fit a thin plate spline and select the weight vector that minimizes 15 the average discrepancy in total fire counts. These methods are consistent with spatio-temporal validation practices (Wikle, 2003).

# 4.1 Point Process Model Feature Selection 

Modeling pyromes as independent allows baseline wildfire intensity and covariate effects to vary across fire regimes, while avoiding the assumption of a single homogeneous process over the entire study region. Table 1 below summarizes wildfire occurence across the ten pyromes during the 1984–2023 study period, and lists pyrome abbreviations used throughout the paper. The summaries show substantial regional variation in average fire activity and extreme monthly counts, with highly active pyromes such as KM contrasting with lower-activity regions such as SNIRHB and CCV. These differences motivate the inclusion of spatial structure in the intensity model. The intensity model includes two distinct classes of covariates. Spatial covariates defined over the domain, S, capture geographic variation in wildfire occurrence and include latitude, longitude, their interaction and quadratic terms, and elevation. These covariates vary across space but are constant in time. We also consider incorporating land use and land cover classifications as spatial covariates. However, these variables are ultimately excluded from the final model because they do not substantially improve predictive performance and introduce several interpretability challenges. In particular, wildfire activity occasionally occurs in seasonally wet areas, leading to misleading associations between water-related classes and fire occurrence. Additionally, while forested regions account for a large proportion of wildfire activity in Northern California overall, a simple indicator for forest cover is not consistently informative across pyromes, especially in less forested regions where other environmental and spatial factors play a larger role. Temporal covariates, defined over the temporal domain T , include large-scale climate conditions 19 that vary from month to month and drive the intensity surface up and down. In addition, monthly harmonic terms specified as cyclical sine and cosine functions of month encode the smooth annual fire seasonality and capture recurring within-year temporal patterns in wildfire activity. Monthly climate variables are spatially averaged over the pyrome domain to produce a single value per month, reflecting region-wide atmospheric and moisture conditions (for example). Numerous ERA5-derived climate variables are considered, including measures of temperature, precipitation, wind speed, humidity, vapor pressure deficit, and drought conditions, together with associated climatologies, anomalies, and lagged values. Many of these variables capture related physical processes and exhibit strong collinearity. Our exploratory analysis of various candidate climate covariates, paired with literature precedent, identified average monthly vapor pressure deficit anomaly (VPDa) as a useful feature for the model (Abatzoglou and Williams, 2016; Williams et al., 2019). # 4.2 Point Process Model Illustration 

Estimation proceeds in two stages. First, parameters controlling the baseline space–time intensity function 𝜆 (𝑠, 𝑡 ) are estimated under an inhomogeneous Poisson process model described in Section 3.2.1. Table 2 reports maximum likelihood estimates, standard errors, and Wald statistics for the KM pyrome. All covariates are standardized prior to fitting allowing effect sizes to be compared on a common scale. Spatial coefficient estimates represent the multiplicative change in baseline intensity from a one standard deviation increase in a covariate, given by exp ( ˆ𝛽 ), while temporal effects similarly scale intensity across all locations at a given time. The fitted baseline model for the KM pyrome identifies several significant spatial and climatic drivers of wildfire occurrence intensity. Monthly VPDa exhibits a positive and statistically sig-nificant association with wildfire occurrence, conditional on the other covariates in the model, 21 indicating that anomalously dry atmospheric conditions correspond to increased wildfire activ-ity. Elevation is negatively associated with wildfire occurrence intensity, suggesting reduced fire activity at higher elevations. Significant longitude and quadratic longitude effects also indicate persistent large-scale spatial gradients across the region. The harmonic seasonal covariates are highly significant, indicating strong annual wildfire sea-sonality throughout all pyromes in Northern California. The sine and cosine terms jointly capture smooth within-year temporal variation, representing the primary wildfire season from approxi-mately June through October or November. Despite capturing broad spatial and seasonal structure, the baseline model does not fully re-produce the observed distribution of monthly wildfire counts displayed in Figure 3. In particular, months of unusually high wildfire counts and periods of unusually low activity occur more fre-quently than expected under a deterministic Poisson intensity surface where the count variance equals the mean. The stochastic shock term addresses this limitation by allowing the intensity to fluctuate randomly through time while retaining the estimated spatial and seasonal structure encoded in 𝜆 (s, 𝑡 ).

Across pyromes, VPDa estimates are generally positive and physically interpretable, reflecting increased wildfire likelihood under anomalously dry atmospheric conditions. However, several 22 pyromes (NSN, SF, NCCFACM, and SNIRHB) produce negative but generally non-significant VPDa coefficients under the baseline specification, likely reflecting residual collinearity, sparse wildfire occurrence, and limitations of the deterministic Poisson intensity surface in capturing localized temporal variability. In the second stage of estimation, a multiplicative shock term 𝑈 𝑡 is introduced to capture overdispersion beyond the Poisson baseline. Months with unusually high counts relative to baseline intensity are common, and the shock term allows additional variance while retaining 𝜆 (𝑠, 𝑡 ) as the baseline structure. Model performance is evaluated using a composite goodness-of-fit score based on distributional summaries of observed monthly counts. The GOF components address Poisson limitations: 𝐷 𝑄

and 𝐷 1 capture low-activity periods, and 𝐷 max ensures accurate reproduction of extreme counts. Together, these metrics evaluate both typical and extreme fire patterns in simulated point patterns. A key advantage of the statistical framework is that it enables explicit uncertainty quantification via simulation. Because the fitted model generates full stochastic realizations of wildfire activity, 30 probabilities of extreme events can be estimated using Monte Carlo methods. Here, we focus on the probability that total annual burned area across Northern California exceeds 685,000 acres, approximately the 90th percentile of the historical distribution. For each year, exceedance probabilities are computed from 10,000 simulated realizations as the fraction exceeding this threshold (Figure 12). Probabilities vary substantially over time, ranging from roughly 3–5% in low-risk years to over 20% in 2023, with most years between 5–10%. This study develops a generative hierarchical statistical modeling framework for wildfire occurrence, burned area, and fire geometry in a unified probabilistic setting. Wildfire counts and locations are modeled as an inhomogeneous Poisson process with a stochastic shock in the intensity to better capture observed mean and variability of fire counts. Given the location of a fire occurrence, the burn pattern is modeled as a randomly-oriented and randomly-sized ellipse. Each component of the model includes physiographic and climate covariates, allowing the framework to generalize across regions with differing climate drivers. A key contribution of the model is its ability to simulate wildfire regimes under observed and future climate scenarios, enabling Monte Carlo approximation of wildfire risk and probabilistic characterization of impacts such as total burned area (Williams et al., 2026). By simulating the full data-generating process, including event locations, temporal intensity, ellipse-based geometries, and burned areas, the model produces empirical distributions of quantities such as total burned area and fire counts. The inclusion of VPDa in both the occurrence and burned area components allows changes in atmospheric dryness to propagate through both fire frequency and size, supporting simulations under changing climate conditions derived from climate models or reanalysis products. These simulations can also serve as inputs to economic, infrastructure, and insurance risk models. Importantly, this simulation-based approach provides a direct representation of uncertainty in wildfire outcomes across spatial and temporal scales. Repeated simulations reveal substantial variability in rare, high-impact events, including extreme fire seasons and large cumulative burned areas. This variability reflects the heavy-tailed nature of wildfire activity and highlights that risk cannot be characterized by mean outcomes alone. By generating ensembles of plausible wildfire realizations, the framework enables direct estimation of extreme-event probabilities and provides a more informative risk assessment than deterministic or point-estimate approaches. The results also highlight meaningful heterogeneity across pyromes. Differences in shock parameters suggest that pyromes vary in how variable wildfire occurrence is over time, in addition 32 to differences in mean intensity and burned-area variance. Pyromes such as KM and EC show relatively higher 𝛾 values in the shock term, consistent with more variable fire activity, while NCCFACM and SNIRHB exhibit lower values, indicating more stable regimes. The burned-area model further reflects this heterogeneity, with KM associated with higher mean burned area, CCV and SNIRHB with substantially lower levels, and additional variation in variance structure across regions. These differences are naturally captured in the Monte Carlo simulations, which preserves region-specific uncertainty rather than imposing a homogeneous risk structure. Several limitations of the current model suggest directions for future research. The elliptical approximation of burned area polygons simplifies complex fire geometries and may underrepresent boundary irregularities, suggesting that more flexible representations of fire shape could improve realism, particularly in heterogeneous terrain or under strong wind-driven spread. The use of temporally independent shock variables captures overdispersion in monthly fire counts but does not reflect persistence or memory in extreme fire conditions, indicating that incorporating higher-frequency meteorological information could help better resolve short-term drivers of wildfire activity and reduce reliance on latent stochasticity. In addition, the set of climate covariates considered in the current specification may not fully capture the range of environmental controls on fire activity, motivating further exploration of alternative or higher-resolution climate variables and temporal aggregation scales. Future work could extend the framework in several directions. More flexible representations of burned area geometry could better capture complex perimeter structure beyond the elliptical approximation, improving realism and allowing fire shapes to respond to environmental features such as land cover. Incorporating dynamic feedback between wildfire occurrence and landscape would allow dependence between successive fire events and evolving fire regimes over time. In addition, integrating higher-frequency meteorological information into the shock process may improve representation of short-term drivers of extreme wildfire activity and reduce reliance on latent stochasticity. Finally, further exploration of alternative climate covariates and temporal aggregation scales could help identify more robust predictors of wildfire activity that are consistently 33 significant across pyromes. Overall, the proposed model demonstrates that a hierarchical stochastic representation of wildfire processes can simultaneously capture spatial occurrence, temporal variability, geometric structure, and burned area dynamics. Most importantly, it provides a coherent framework for Monte Carlo approximation of wildfire risk, enabling full uncertainty quantification of both typical and extreme fire outcomes. This represents a substantive step toward probabilistic wildfire modeling that is directly aligned with risk assessment and decision-making under uncertainty.
