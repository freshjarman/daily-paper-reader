Abstract The spatial distribution of waiting pedestrians has two primary drivers: environ-mental preference and interaction with other pedestrians. Spatial point processes naturally capture both spatial heterogeneity and repulsive interaction. In previous work (Sickert Karam et al., arXiv:2606.14532, 2026), we proposed a Gibbs model with inhomogeneous intensity and a modified Diggle–Gates–Stibbard interaction function, which reproduces many phenomena in replicated patterns of pedestrians waiting at a train station. Its single interaction function acts as an effective interaction, averaging over behavioral regimes such as interactions within social groups and among strangers. In this article, we show how groups affect distance-based summary statistics and take first steps towards account-ing for them. We detect groups from pairwise distances and contact durations, replace each group by a “macro-pedestrian” at its average position, and refit the model. This yields a larger interaction range and better agreement in nearest-neighbor statistics than the original model, although the two fits concern different datasets and centroid-based distances likely overstate repulsion. This first attempt thus resolves some discrepancies but falls short of a fully adequate solution, opening avenues for further research.

## 1 Introduction 

Understanding how pedestrians wait on train station platforms, and modeling the spatial patterns formed by their positions, is highly relevant for the design and operation of trans-port infrastructure [1]. These patterns are driven primarily by two aspects: environmental preferences and interactions between pedestrians. Abstracting the pedestrians as points in the Euclidean plane leads to a natural modeling choice for these arrangements as a realization of a spatial point process [2], a stochastic model that can capture both back-ground effects and interactions [3]. Waiting pedestrians exhibit repulsive interactions, as on the one hand, the distance between individuals is inherently limited by their physical size, and on the other hand, pedestrians may additionally prefer to keep a social distance from one another [4]. In previous work [2], we proposed a Gibbs point process model [5] with inhomogeneous intensity and a modified Diggle-Gates-Stibbard interaction function [2, 6], which quantitatively reproduces many features of waiting crowds. In this model, all pairwise interactions are described by a single interaction function, which can be inter-preted as an effective interaction averaging over different behavioral regimes present in a crowd. Such regimes include interactions between strangers and those between members of social groups, which commonly occur in pedestrian crowds [7], so that short distances between individuals may reflect either weak global repulsion or membership of the same group. We build on this framework in the present work by explicitly accounting for the presence of groups and proposing first steps toward addressing their influence on the in-teraction structure of waiting pedestrians. We begin by illustrating how groups affect distance-based summary statistics, and in-troduce a relatively simple correction based on the concept of “macro-pedestrians” – re-placing each group by a point at its average position (see Fig. 1 for an example). Then, the Gibbs model is refit to the resulting patterns. This approach substantially increases SPPs for Waiting Pedestrians with Groups 3

the interaction range of the fitted model, while making the transition between interaction regimes (from full exclusion to independence) smoother. Moreover, simulations show that this new model fits the macro-pedestrian patterns better in terms of nearest-neighbor statistics than the original model [2] fits the patterns of individual pedestrians. This com-parison should be interpreted with care, however, as the two models are fitted to and evaluated on different datasets: after correcting for groups, the macro-pedestrian patterns contain fewer points and no longer include distances within groups. Furthermore, we argue that reducing groups to their centroid introduces new biases, as distances between singletons and groups are measured from group centers rather than their edges, favoring long ranges of interaction. This simple correction thus resolves some discrepancies but falls short of being a full-fledged solution, opening avenues for further research. This paper is organized as follows. Sec. 2 describes the dataset, consisting of repli-cated spatial patterns (i.e. arrangements of points hypothesized to be generated by the same underlying process) of pedestrian positions collected at Eindhoven central station in the Netherlands. Additionally, the group detection methodology is discussed. In Sec. 3, the Gibbs model is introduced, as well as the methodology used for estimation of the un-derlying inhomogeneous background intensity and the interaction parameters. In Sec. 4, results of estimation under consideration of groups are presented and contrasted with re-sults obtained from the model ignoring groups. A discussion in Sec. 5 closes the work. 

## 2 Waiting Pedestrian Data, Group Detection, and Aggregation 

In this section, we present the dataset used for later analysis. It consists of configurations of waiting pedestrians at a train station, each a static snapshot in time. These snapshots can be viewed as replicated spatial patterns [3], approximately independent realizations of a spatial point process. Crucially, this setting incorporates the combination of an inhomo-geneous background intensity, i.e. attractiveness of different positions, and an interaction between points. This creates a unique statistical challenge, as it is inherently difficult to separate these two influences during estimation [2, 3]. As a baseline, we use the dataset of waiting pedestrian configurations introduced in [2], extracted from overhead sensor recordings on Platform 2 of Eindhoven Central Station in the Netherlands. This data is publicly available (with only minor discrepancies, described in [2]) at [8]. Pedestrian trajectories are recorded at 10 Hz and classified as waiting or 

passing using a velocity-based criterion. Configurations of waiting individuals are sam-pled one minute before peaks in platform occupancy, yielding replicated spatial patterns of high-occupancy scenarios while reducing the direct influence of train arrivals. The platform exhibits spatial heterogeneity due to entrances, obstacles, buildings, and seating areas. See [2] for a more detailed description of the geometry and preprocessing. At this point, we deviate from the dataset of [2] (we will refer to this as the original dataset ) by considering social groups. group-detection algorithm identifies pairs of pedestrians that remain close to each other for a large fraction of the time during which both are observed, and then takes connected components of the resulting contact graph as social groups. For each pair of pedestrians, the time tcontact for which the pedestrians are “in contact,” i.e. the number of frames where the distance is below a certain threshold (we choose 1 .5 m, aligning with [7, 9]), and the time tpersonal for which pedestrians are within “personal distance,” set at 1 m, are recorded. Pairs are then checked for two relations within a timeframe of 30 s 1 leading up to time of the snapshot being captured: 

tcontact ≥ a · tsimultaneous ; tpersonal ≥ b · tsimultaneous ,

where tsimultaneous is the time in which both pedestrians are simultaneously in frame. If both conditions hold for predetermined values of a, b, the pair is determined to belong to the same social group. We choose a = 0.85 and b = 0.4 as proposed and validated in [7], as the scenario tested there closely resembles our own. See Fig. 2 for some sum-mary statistics of groups in the dataset, showing small group sizes are substantially more common than large ones, and how group size is related to platform occupancy. Snapshots containing groups larger than 5 members are then excluded for further analysis, as their centroid may lie far away from the positions of the pedestrians at the edge of the group. We introduce the notation Ngroupmax to denote the maximum size of a group in the dataset, i.e. here Ngroupmax = 5. This can artificially inflate nearest-neighbor distances in the full pattern, disproportionately affecting distance-based diagnostics later on. This filtering leaves us with 2457 snapshots, compared to 2629 snapshots in the original dataset of [2], see Fig. 1(a) for an example before aggregation. Each detected group present in a snap-shot is then represented by its centroid, while singleton pedestrians are left unchanged, leaving us with a dataset consisting of individuals and macro-pedestrians representing groups. We use the semiparametric estimation algorithm of [2]. In brief, an initial kernel estimate of the intensity function β is obtained under a Poisson assumption (i.e. no interaction between points), on the basis of which interaction parameters θ are estimated by condi-tional maximum pseudolikelihood. Having obtained parameters, the bandwidth for the kernel estimate of β is modified by likelihood cross-validation over a set of rescalings of the original bandwidth. Crucially, the likelihood used for the cross-validation is the Gibbs likelihood with the parameter estimates for the interaction function plugged in. The final model is obtained by re-estimating the intensity function and maximizing the conditional pseudolikelihood again using the new estimate. This procedure can be iterated.

Nonetheless, comparing performance to the model without grouping comes with some caveats. We are no longer comparing two models to the same dataset, as one con-tains the aggregated macro-pedestrians, while the other does not. Strictly speaking, the evidence only supports the conclusion that the DGS model fits the macro-pedestrian data more closely than it fits the uncorrected data. Overall, correcting for groups can lead to the model having a better fit than on the uncorrected data. However, the considerable change in the choice of interaction radii indicates that estimates may be biased in the opposite direction from [2] (here, experi-ments showed that the estimation method tends to underestimate the strength or range of repulsion). The aggregation step removes short within-group distances entirely, replac-ing multiple pedestrian positions by a single centroid. This changes the geometry of the pattern, as distances from the group to nearby pedestrians are measured from the centroid rather than from the closest group member. ## 5 Discussion 

We examined how social groups affect inference for repulsive spatial point process mod-els of waiting pedestrians and took first steps towards accounting for them. Starting from the model and semiparametric estimation procedure of [2], we detected groups based on pairwise distances and contact durations, replaced each group by a macro-pedestrian at its centroid, and refitted the modified DGS interaction function to the resulting pat-terns. This correction yielded a better fit in terms of nearest-neighbor statistics, yet two caveats remain. First, a direct comparison with the group-agnostic model is difficult, as the macro-pedestrian correction changes the underlying data used for estimation and val-idation. Second, the correction may cause the estimated interaction range to overshoot. The main result concerns the nearest-neighbor statistics. Simulations under the refitted model reproduce the NN and 2-NN distance distributions of the macro-pedestrian data well at all three occupancy levels, whereas the group-agnostic model shows a pronounced short-range peak, likely caused by pairs. The better fit is accompanied by a considerably larger estimated interaction radius R, while the shape parameter α decreases, resulting in a more gradual transition from full exclusion to independence over a longer range. This suggests that proximity within groups accounts for part of the short-range repulsion inferred from the raw data. This improvement must be interpreted with care, since the macro-pedestrian correction changes the data used for estimation and validation. Aggre-gation reduces the number of points and shifts the occupancy distribution towards lower levels (Tab. 1), and snapshots containing groups of more than five members were excluded altogether, since their centroids can lie far from the group members. Overall, this makes a direct comparison of the goodness of fit difficult. As mentioned above, we conjecture that positional averaging overcorrects the interac-tion range R. Replacing a group by its centroid removes distance structures within groups entirely, and measures distances to neighboring pedestrians from the center rather than from the nearest group member. The aggregated patterns therefore appear more regular than physical configurations, and the estimation procedure, which tended to underestimate repulsion when groups were ignored [2], may now be biased in the opposite direction. Po-sitional averaging approach is thus a simple first step to address the influence of groups, but it falls short of a fully adequate treatment. This opens the door to further, more detailed investigation of modeling social groups in waiting pedestrians. Rather than aggregating the data, social groups could be represented within the model itself, so that all models are fitted to the same individual-level data and can be compared directly. Marked point processes [3] are promising in this regard, as they allow the interaction to depend on the marks of both points. With marks encoding group membership, pairs within the same group and pairs of strangers could follow sepa-rate interaction mechanisms. Alternatively, the macro-pedestrian approach proposed here could be refined by treating macro-pedestrians as disks whose diameters reflect the spatial size of their groups, with interaction depending on the distance between disk boundaries rather than centroids. This could reduce the bias introduced by positional averaging, al-though representing pedestrians (and groups) as disks remains an approximation which is not necessarily realistic, particularly for large groups. 12 L.
